Oops, Your Files Have Been Encrypted

How a ransomware operator's implementation mistakes transformed an apparently unrecoverable encrypted archive into a fully recoverable dataset. This investigation followed the trail from intermittent encryption and ZIP recovery all the way to keystream reconstruction, full archive recovery, and a hidden secondary challenge concealed inside the encrypted data.

#dfir #ransomware #cryptanalysis #forensics #datarecovery #incidentresponse

Executive Summary

When I first opened the challenge, it looked like a fairly typical ransomware recovery scenario. There was an encrypted ZIP archive, a disk image containing encrypted files, and a hint pointing toward intermittent encryption.

What made the challenge interesting wasn't the encryption algorithm itself. Instead, it was the series of operational mistakes made during the implementation of the ransomware.

By exploiting those mistakes, I was able to reconstruct the encryption keystream, decrypt multiple protected files, recover a hidden challenge, and eventually restore the entire archive.

The encryption wasn't broken. The implementation was.

Initial Observations

The first thing that caught my attention was the challenge tag: 

#intermittent_encryption

That immediately changed how I approached the investigation. Instead of focusing on decryption, I started thinking about recovery.

Intermittent encryption is fundamentally different from traditional ransomware encryption. Rather than encrypting an entire file, only selected portions are encrypted while large sections remain untouched.

From a forensic perspective, that creates opportunity.

If enough structural information survives, recovery may become possible even without possessing the original encryption key.

The First Crack in the Ransomware

The encrypted archive became my first target.

Rather than attempting to decrypt it immediately, I wanted to understand how much of the original ZIP structure remained intact.

ZIP Structure Recovery
zip -FF PD12M_dataset.zip.enc --out repaired.zip

I wasn't expecting complete recovery. I only wanted to determine whether enough ZIP metadata survived to reveal what had originally existed inside the archive.

The results were better than expected.

Multiple JPEG files became accessible and, more importantly, one recovered file stood out from everything else. 

085 - A Key Too Familiar(ko,en).docx

That filename felt less like user content and more like a clue left behind for investigators.

A Challenge Hidden Inside Another Challenge

Most recovered files were images. One wasn't.

Buried among the JPEG files was the recovered document named: 

085 - A Key Too Familiar(ko,en).docx

After opening it, I discovered that it contained an entirely separate forensic challenge.

At that point, the investigation split into two tracks: recovering the original archive and solving the secondary challenge hidden inside the recovered data.

Following the Keystream

The document hinted that the ransomware relied on a stream cipher.

That observation immediately suggested one important possibility: keystream reuse.

If the same keystream had been used across multiple files, recovering that keystream once could allow recovery everywhere else.

The breakthrough came from a file inside the disk image: 

node-v22.16.0-x64.msi.enc

Unlike the other encrypted files, I could obtain a pristine copy of the original installer directly from NodeJS.

That changed the entire problem.

Known plaintext is one of the most dangerous things a stream cipher can encounter. Once plaintext and ciphertext become available together, recovering the keystream becomes possible.

The Moment Everything Changed

Stream ciphers rely on XOR operations.

That means: 

Ciphertext XOR Plaintext = Keystream

By XORing the encrypted NodeJS installer with the original installer, I recovered the exact keystream used by the ransomware.

At that moment, the challenge stopped being about ransomware analysis.

It became a keystream recovery problem.

Decrypting FILE_A Through FILE_H

Using the recovered keystream, I extracted and decrypted the encrypted files located on the Desktop.

The recovered contents were merged together into a single output file, revealing additional clues about the attack.

Near the end of the recovered text was a Base64-encoded string. 

T29vcHMsIGZsYWch

After decoding: 

Ooops, flag!

The secondary challenge had been solved.

The Ransomware's Fatal Mistakes

Mistake #1 — Intermittent Encryption

The ransomware encrypted only portions of the ZIP archive. This preserved enough metadata and file structure to allow recovery tools to rebuild sections of the archive.

Mistake #2 — Keystream Reuse

The same stream cipher keystream was reused across multiple files. Once recovered from a known plaintext pair, it could be applied elsewhere.

Mistake #3 — Predictable Known Plaintext

Encrypting publicly available software introduced a perfect recovery scenario because clean copies could be downloaded and compared against the encrypted versions.

Finding the Pattern

While analyzing the recovered keystream, I noticed something unusual.

The data wasn't random.

The keystream appeared to repeat every 4096 bytes.

Initially this looked insignificant, but the pattern became increasingly important when attempting to recover the remaining encrypted images.

Further analysis revealed that encryption alignment depended heavily on the positioning of null-byte regions inside the repeating keystream.

Once the relationship between the repeating blocks and null-byte regions became clear, reconstructing the remaining encrypted portions became significantly easier.

Brute-Forcing Alignment

The images between 020.jpg and 028.jpg represented the most difficult recovery segment.

To identify where decryption should begin, I searched for known JPEG filename structures and ZIP metadata patterns.

Instead of guessing, I brute-forced offsets until the recovered output began producing valid archive structures.

When the expected magic bytes finally appeared, I knew the alignment was correct.

The investigation wasn't really about breaking encryption. It was about finding where the ransomware's assumptions no longer matched reality.

Full Archive Recovery

With the correct alignment identified, I reconstructed the entire archive and repeatedly validated recovered ZIP structures.

Several candidate archives were generated during testing, but only one produced a fully extractable archive with valid CRC checks across all files.

The final recovery yielded:

  • 100 JPEG images
  • The embedded DOCX challenge
  • Valid ZIP structure
  • Successful CRC verification

The ransomware's recovery-resistant design had ultimately failed because of implementation flaws rather than cryptographic weaknesses.

Lessons Learned

  • Intermittent encryption creates recovery opportunities.
  • Known plaintext remains a major threat to stream ciphers.
  • Keystream reuse can completely undermine encryption.
  • Archive metadata often survives longer than attackers expect.
  • Recovery frequently depends more on implementation mistakes than cryptographic failures.

Final Thoughts

What I enjoyed most about this challenge was that recovery didn't come from defeating the encryption algorithm itself.

Every breakthrough came from understanding how the ransomware operated, identifying where its assumptions failed, and leveraging those failures to recover information that should have been inaccessible.

The ZIP archive revealed the first clues. The keystream revealed the rest. And by the end of the investigation, the challenge wasn't really about ransomware anymore.

It was about how a handful of implementation mistakes can completely undermine an otherwise effective encryption scheme.
