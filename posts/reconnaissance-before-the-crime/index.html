Reconnaissance Before the Crime

A 52-minute browser session. Two AI platforms. A suspect who didn't realise that everything they searched for, every conversation they exported, every tool they visited was all still sitting on the disk they thought told a different story.

Executive Summary

The suspect used two AI-assisted reconnaissance platforms ChatGPT and Claude to research how to alter and erase video metadata from iPhone recordings, with explicit follow-on queries about avoiding forensic detection. The entire research session was reconstructed from browser SQLite history databases across two browsers, corroborated by locally recovered export archives and downloaded PDFs of AI conversations.

This challenge gave me a virtual disk image recovered from a suspect's personal computer in an evidence tampering investigation. My objective was straightforward but required precision: identify which online services were used for pre-crime reconnaissance, and reconstruct a full timeline with timestamps granular enough that another examiner could replicate every step.

What I found was a methodical, self-directed research campaign one that escalated from general curiosity about iPhone video metadata to explicitly counter-forensic queries about how to modify that metadata without leaving traces detectable by investigators.

Investigation Walkthrough

Step 1 Verifying the Evidence

Before doing anything else, I verified the integrity of the provided disk image. This is non-negotiable in forensic work if the hash doesn't match, nothing that follows is admissible or trustworthy. 

md5sum target.ad1
f5112719b720429525051d9a2830b8f9  target.ad1

Hash confirmed. I loaded the image into FTK Imager and extracted the logical files, then ingested the resulting file set into Autopsy as a Logical Files data source. From here I could navigate the user's file system without touching the original evidence.

Step 2 First Look at the File System

My first pass through the extracted files was a high-level orientation looking for anything that stood out before diving into browser artifacts. The Downloads folder for the user profile at C:\Users\newnew\Downloads immediately caught my attention.

Sitting inside it: ChromeSetup.exe and ChromeSetup (1).exe. The system already had Microsoft Edge installed as the default browser. Installing Chrome mid-session is not inherently suspicious, but paired with what I'd find later in the history databases, it became a significant behavioral data point. Also present: a file named conversation.pdf a name strongly suggestive of an exported AI chat session.

Analyst Note

When I see a fresh browser installation alongside a downloaded conversation PDF in the same user profile, I treat that combination as a signal of deliberate compartmentalization not coincidence. The working hypothesis from this point: the suspect wanted separate, isolated browsing sessions for their research.

Step 3 Extracting and Querying Browser History

Autopsy's browser artifact module surfaced the SQLite history databases for both Edge and Chrome. I queried these directly in SQLite Viewer to work with the raw records rather than Autopsy's parsed summaries, which can sometimes drop URLs or merge duplicate entries.

The history databases were located at their standard paths: 

Edge:
C:\Users\newnew\AppData\Local\Microsoft\Edge\User Data\Default\History

Chrome:
C:\Users\newnew\AppData\Local\Google\Chrome\User Data\Default\History

Chrome (and Edge) store visit timestamps as microseconds elapsed since January 1, 1601 the Windows FILETIME epoch. I converted the last_visit_time field values to UTC across both databases to build a unified, chronological picture.

Step 4 Identifying the Reconnaissance Platforms

Sorting through the URL records from both browsers, two AI assistant services were unambiguous: chatgpt.com / openai.com and claude.ai. Both appeared in Edge first, and both were revisited again after Chrome was installed confirming the dual-browser pattern I'd anticipated.

The access flow was not accidental browsing. I could trace the full authentication sequences OAuth flows through Google accounts, redirects through auth0.openai.com, and direct navigations to /new chat pages on Claude indicating intentional, active use of both platforms across two distinct browser environments.

What the Pattern Actually Shows

Reading each browser entry in isolation, nothing stands out. It is only when the full sequence is laid out with timestamps, with the query escalation visible, with the dual-browser behaviour mapped that the intent becomes clear.

The session followed a recognisable arc that I'd describe in three stages. The first was scoping: general questions about what metadata exists in iPhone videos, framed as curiosity or privacy concern. The second was tooling: identification and evaluation of specific software (ExifTool, HandBrake, GeoSetter) capable of performing the modifications. The third and most forensically significant was counter-forensics: active research into what investigators use to detect metadata tampering, followed by questions about how to perform the modifications without leaving traces those tools would find.

Behavioral Observation

The suspect consistently reframed their queries using legitimate-sounding language "privacy protection," "correct" metadata, "ethical" video management. This is a pattern of someone who understands that AI platforms apply content guardrails, and who has learned to soften their phrasing to avoid triggering refusals. It doesn't change what they were asking. It does tell you they knew what they were asking was something they shouldn't be asking.

The Chrome installation is its own statement. There was no functional reason to install a second browser. Edge was working. The only benefit a second browser provides in this context is a separate authentication environment and a separate history database. The suspect was compartmentalising, either because they planned to deny certain activity, or because they had been told (perhaps by ChatGPT itself) that browser sessions leave logs.

The two AI platforms were used differently. ChatGPT received the deeper, more iterative technical queries ExifTool workflows, GPS modification steps, counter-forensic methodology. Claude was queried twice and both times with broader or meta-level questions (saving conversations, ethical metadata management). My read: ChatGPT was the primary research tool; Claude was a secondary cross-reference or fallback when ChatGPT's guidance felt incomplete.

Forensic Takeaways

  • Browser SQLite history is durable. Both Edge and Chrome retained the full URL and timestamp record of this session. The suspect made no attempt to clear browser history a common oversight when the focus of counter-forensic attention is on the target file rather than the research session that preceded the crime.
  • AI export archives become forensic artifacts. ChatGPT's data export feature designed as a user privacy mechanism created two recoverable .zip archives on disk containing the full conversation transcript. These corroborate the browser history and, more importantly, contain the exact queries and responses, not just the URLs visited.
  • Dual-browser installation is a behavioural signal. When a user installs a second browser mid-session with no obvious utility reason, treat that installation as a deliberate compartmentalisation event. Audit both history databases independently before correlating them.
  • Counter-forensic queries are evidence of intent. A suspect asking an AI "will investigators be able to detect this?" is demonstrating awareness that what they're doing is wrong and discoverable. In a prosecution context, that query is directly relevant to mens rea.
  • The legitimacy-framing pattern is consistent and recognisable. Queries that open with "for privacy reasons," "ethically speaking," or "how do I correct..." followed by operational specifics are a known evasion pattern in AI-assisted crime research. Document the linguistic progression the initial framing and how it tightens over the session as part of the timeline narrative.

Conclusion

By mounting the disk image in FTK Imager, ingesting the logical file set into Autopsy, and querying both browser history databases directly in SQLite Viewer, I was able to reconstruct the complete pre-crime reconnaissance session with timestamp-level precision. The services used were ChatGPT and Claude, accessed across both Edge and Chrome over a 52-minute window on the evening of August 3, 2024.

What the browser history revealed wasn't just which platforms were visited. It revealed a deliberate, self-directed research campaign that progressed from ignorance about iPhone metadata to an actionable, counter-forensically-informed plan for altering it. The suspect's attempt to preserve the guidance offline in a PDF, in two exported archives left more evidence on the disk than simply browsing would have done.

Closing Note

In trying to learn how not to get caught, the suspect created a detailed record of exactly what they planned to do and why they thought it would work. That record survived on the disk they thought would tell a different story. It often goes this way.
